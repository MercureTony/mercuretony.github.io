I recently stumbled on [Tom Brady's commencement speech at Georgetown University](https://youtu.be/ntsn7wCZyuA). I'm not a huge American football fan, but a story he told has stayed with me.

![Tom Brady's Georgetown commencement address, shown in the original video.](https://img.youtube.com/vi/ntsn7wCZyuA/hqdefault.jpg "Tom Brady's 2026 Georgetown commencement speech. Source: Georgetown University / YouTube.")

In the 2017 Super Bowl, the New England Patriots were losing 28–3 against the Atlanta Falcons. Brady recalled a point when their estimated probability of losing was **99.7%**.

I had to think about that number for a moment. If you were watching, you could hardly be blamed for turning off the television. But they kept playing. The Patriots went on to win 34–28 in overtime.

What interested me wasn't just the comeback. It was that everyone involved had to make their decisions *before* knowing the ending. We watch that game today knowing the Patriots win. They had no such reassurance.

[Watch the speech that prompted this reflection](https://youtu.be/ntsn7wCZyuA).

## Resilience

> **Resilience:** The capacity to adapt to adversity, recover from setbacks, and keep functioning or moving forward despite difficult circumstances.

I've been thinking about what that word actually asks of us. We often use it when somebody succeeds after a difficult period, but I wonder whether we're giving the eventual outcome too much credit.

Growing up, I watched Cristiano Ronaldo all the time. What I admired wasn't only the goals. It was his appetite for the work: the training, the expectations he placed on himself, the way he returned after disappointing performances.

In 2019, Juventus went into the second leg against Atlético Madrid trailing 2–0 on aggregate. Ronaldo scored three times and Juventus advanced. It was one of those nights that reminds you why sport can be so compelling.

But if Juventus had lost that night, would Ronaldo's years of discipline suddenly have counted for less? I don't think so. That's the trouble with judging resilience only by its most spectacular outcomes.

## What We Don't See in the Success Story

I think about Elon Musk in 2008. SpaceX had failed to reach orbit on three attempts, while Tesla was also under severe financial pressure. The fourth Falcon 1 launch succeeded, but that result wasn't assured when the team prepared for it.

Today it's tempting to tell the story backwards. We know SpaceX survived. We know it went on to accomplish remarkable things. That knowledge makes the earlier uncertainty look almost inevitable, as if each failure were merely a chapter on the way to success.

It didn't feel inevitable to the people living it.

![Elon Musk overlooking wreckage of a SpaceX test vehicle.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Elon_Musk_overlooking_the_remains_of_F9R_%2836411051713%29.jpg "Elon Musk looking at F9R debris in 2014, not the Falcon 1 failures of 2008. Photo: Steve Jurvetson, CC BY 2.0.")

I find this photograph especially striking. The photo is from **2014**, years after the 2008 crisis, but it captures something about building things in the real world: even after a breakthrough, there are still failures to confront.

And there's a selection problem in the stories we tell. We hear from the people whose fourth launch worked. We hear less often from those who tried just as hard and ran out of money, time or luck. Perseverance improves our chances in some circumstances; it doesn't entitle us to a happy ending.

## When Should We Stop?

This is where the simple advice to *never give up* starts to bother me.

Imagine running a company for five years without finding customers who want what you're building. Is the courageous thing to carry on unchanged for another five? Maybe not. Perhaps the right thing is to question the idea, listen more carefully and build something different.

Ernest Shackleton offers an example I keep returning to. He set out to cross Antarctica, but his ship, *Endurance*, was trapped and eventually crushed by ice. He did not finish the crossing. He changed the objective: bring the crew home. All 28 men survived.

Would we call that giving up? He abandoned the original plan, certainly. But he didn't abandon the people whose lives depended on him.

That distinction matters to me. There's a difference between being attached to a purpose and being attached to a method. When things go wrong, we sometimes need to change the second to protect the first.

## Before the Final Whistle

One thing sports gives us that life doesn't is a clock. Brady knew there was time left in the game. He didn't know if his team could come back, but he knew what a next play looked like.

Outside a stadium, the uncertainty is much harder. There is no scoreboard that tells us how close we are to making progress. An effort that looks promising today may fail next year. Something that appears finished may have a second life after we rethink it.

I've written before about the storms we encounter in life and the importance of pressing forward. I still believe that. But I understand *pressing forward* a little differently now. It isn't always about working harder, or pretending not to be exhausted. Sometimes it's about resting, asking for help, admitting that an approach isn't working, and deciding what remains worth pursuing.

Brady's 99.7% caught my attention because it showed how wrong a nearly certain prediction can turn out to be. Not because every 0.3% chance deserves our time or resources. A low probability is still a low probability. But odds alone can't tell us everything about whether an effort is meaningful.

I don't know how many difficult chapters in life end in a comeback. Probably fewer than we would like. Still, I don't want to mistake today's position for the end of the story before I've properly considered what I can do next.

**Being behind doesn't always mean you've lost.**

*The game isn't over yet.*
