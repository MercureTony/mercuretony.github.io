For most of my life, I quietly believed that being different was a problem to be solved. Not loudly—I never said it out loud—but it lived underneath my choices. I noticed the ways I didn't quite fit, the questions I asked that no one else seemed to be asking, the paths I was drawn to that no one around me was walking. And some part of me assumed that if I just adjusted enough, smoothed enough edges, I would eventually arrive at *normal*—that place where everyone else seemed to already live comfortably.

It took me a long time to realize that this place doesn't exist. The crowd I was trying to blend into was made up entirely of people who, on the inside, felt exactly as different as I did.

> "*I praise you because I am fearfully and wonderfully made.*" – Psalm 139:14 (NIV)

We are not mass-produced. We talk about humanity as if we came off an assembly line—same parts, same design, minor cosmetic variations. But the truth is closer to the opposite. No two people share the same combination of memories, wounds, gifts, fears, and longings. The version of you reading this has never existed before and will never exist again. Difference isn't your defect. It's your signature.

So why does being different feel so heavy?

## The illusion of the crowd

A lot of our anxiety about being different comes from a quiet miscalculation: we compare our inside to everyone else's outside. We see the polished surface—the confident posture, the easy laughter, the seeming certainty—and we assume that behind it is a person who has it all figured out, who belongs effortlessly. Meanwhile, we're aware of our own doubts, our own strangeness, our own sense of not-quite-fitting.

But everyone is doing this at the same time. The "normal" people you're measuring yourself against are measuring themselves against you. The crowd is an optical illusion. There is no homogeneous mass of well-adjusted, identical people that you've been excluded from. There's just a lot of individuals, each quietly convinced that they're the odd one out.

Once you see this clearly, the pressure changes shape. You're not failing to fit into something real. You're trying to dissolve into something that was never there.

## Conformity costs more than it looks

The instinct to fit in isn't irrational. It's ancient. For most of human history, belonging to the group meant survival, and being cast out meant danger. That wiring is still in us. The fear of standing out is not weakness—it's inheritance.

But the price of that wiring, left unexamined, is steep. When you spend your energy shrinking the parts of yourself that don't match, you don't just hide them—you starve them. The very things that make you distinct, the things that could become your contribution to the world, get filed down until they're unrecognizable. You end up trading the one thing only you can offer for the temporary comfort of being unremarkable.

And here's the quiet irony: the people we admire most, the ones whose lives we point to, are almost never the ones who fit in best. They're the ones who, at some point, stopped apologizing for being different and started building from it.

## Different is the raw material of purpose

I've come to believe that the things that make you different are not obstacles to your purpose—they *are* your purpose, in raw form. The question that won't leave you alone. The problem you can't stop noticing. The way you see something everyone else walks past. These aren't bugs in your design. They're the early shape of the work you're meant to do.

If you were exactly like everyone else, you would have nothing distinct to give. It's precisely the places where you diverge that hold your potential contribution. The world doesn't move forward through people who blend in perfectly. It moves forward through people who saw differently and were willing to act on it.

Look at the rest of creation and the pattern is everywhere. God made the animals, and not one of them apologizes for being what it is. They come in every size and shape—the whale and the hummingbird, the elephant and the ant. They live in wildly different habitats: the depths of the ocean, the heights of the sky, the heat of the desert, the cold of the poles. Each one is distinct, and each one fits perfectly into its role. The eagle was never meant to swim, and the fish was never meant to fly. Their differences aren't flaws in the design—they *are* the design. If the God who made all of that built such deliberate variety into every creature, why would we assume He made us to be copies of one another?

That doesn't mean being different is comfortable. It often means being misunderstood for a while. It means walking before there's a path. But discomfort and wrongness are not the same thing. You can be uncomfortable and still be exactly where you're supposed to be.

## When conformity wears a religious mask

The pressure to be the same doesn't only come from the world. Sometimes it comes dressed in religious language, which makes it harder to question. I've heard, in some Christian circles, that a believer must laugh a certain way, or shouldn't go to restaurants too often because it's "mundane," along with a long list of other unwritten rules about how a *real* Christian looks and sounds and spends their time. The intentions may be sincere, but the effect is the opposite of the gospel. It quietly teaches that holiness means becoming identical—that to follow Christ is to file down everything that makes you *you* until you match the group.

But that defeats the whole point. If God built such deliberate variety into every creature, He didn't suddenly change His mind when He made people who love Him. The body of Christ is described as having many parts precisely because they are *not* the same—the hand is not the foot, the eye is not the ear, and the body needs all of them. A faith that flattens everyone into one acceptable personality isn't producing holiness; it's producing copies. And it robs the community of the very differences that were meant to serve it.

The same goes for marriage, which I think is one of the most misunderstood relationships of all. I would argue that the purpose of marriage is not to make your spouse look more like *you*—same preferences, same habits, same opinions—but to help them look more like Christ. Those are very different goals. One tries to erase difference; the other honors it while pointing it upward. You're not there to sand your partner down into a copy of yourself. You're there to help them become the fullest, most Christlike version of who *they* already are.

## Normal was never the goal

If I could go back and tell my younger self one thing, it would be this: stop waiting to become normal. It's not coming, and you wouldn't want it if it did. The goal was never to disappear into the average. The goal is to become *fully* yourself—edges and all—and to offer that to the world without flinching.

Being different isn't a sign that something went wrong. It's a sign that you're a person and not a copy. It's not the exception to the human condition. It's the most ordinary thing about being human.

So if you've ever felt like you don't quite fit—like there's some standard everyone else met that you somehow missed—I want you to hear this plainly:

It's okay to be different.

It's actually normal.
