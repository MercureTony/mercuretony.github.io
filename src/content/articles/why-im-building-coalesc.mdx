*"Too much of the work is still manual."*

That's the sentence on the screen behind me (Figure 1). At Coalesc, we're working on a specific part of that problem: the preparation that turns scattered client information into accounting work someone else can review and continue.

![Anthony on stage in front of a slide that reads "Too much of the work is still manual," listing collect documents, figure out what's missing, check the numbers, move information between systems and prepare the file for review, with a seated audience in the foreground.](/writings/why-im-building-coalesc/stage.jpg "Presenting at Canada Fintech Forum in Montréal, September 15, 2026: the manual work behind every accounting file.")

Consider a client preparing their personal tax return who owns rental properties.

They send receipts, insurance documents and property tax statements. An accountant still needs to open those documents, identify the relevant year, determine which property each expense belongs to and assemble the information into a usable schedule.

If something is unclear, there is another question for the client. When the answer arrives, someone needs to connect it to the right file and resume the work.

Receiving the documents is one step in a much longer process.

The work continues after the documents are organized. In a year-end file, someone still needs to connect balances to supporting information, investigate differences and prepare the necessary updates. A statement covers the wrong period. A difference needs an explanation. If a question surfaces during review, the file may return to the preparer, who may need another answer from the client.

One correction can then reach across the whole file: a section to update, taxes to recalculate, and the return, the provision and the financial statements to bring back in line.

When a gap surfaces late, the work stops. Someone starts, discovers something missing, sets the file aside and later has to rebuild their understanding before they can continue. A colleague picking it up needs to know what has already been checked and what remains unresolved.

These tasks require care. They also consume time before the accountant can make the decisions the client is relying on them to make. And because every additional client brings more of this preparation and coordination, it limits how much work a firm can take on.

Coming from engineering and data, I'm interested in how we can turn more of this repeatable preparation into software.

A difficult part is preserving context as the work moves forward. A document belongs to a particular client, period and engagement. A new answer may resolve one question and raise another. The person reviewing the file needs to understand how the result was produced.

That is what we're building toward with Coalesc, starting with tax, compilation and year-end preparation: carrying out more of the repeatable work, keeping the supporting evidence connected, and making unresolved issues visible early enough for accountants to act on them.

Our ambition is for software to present that work with its supporting evidence, outstanding questions and exceptions. Accountants would then have a clearer starting point for review, judgment and approval.

We have to earn that starting point. A useful test is how much preparation an accountant can accept, and how much they still need to correct or reconstruct.

Working through real files with accounting teams helps us make that distinction. It reveals the details a clean demonstration can miss.

For a firm, getting this right could mean handling more client work without preparation effort growing at the same pace. It could also make handing a file to a colleague easier, because the reasoning and unresolved questions travel with it.

That's the problem I'm spending my time on: helping accounting teams move from information received to work they can confidently take forward.
