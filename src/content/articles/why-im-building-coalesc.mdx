*"Too much of the work is still manual."*

That's the sentence on the screen behind me (Figure 1). It's also the simplest way to explain what [Coalesc](https://coalesc.ai) is for.

![Anthony on stage in front of a slide that reads "Too much of the work is still manual," listing collect documents, figure out what's missing, check the numbers, move information between systems and prepare the file for review, with a seated audience in the foreground.](/writings/why-im-building-coalesc/stage.jpg "Presenting at Canada Fintech Forum in Montréal, September 15, 2026.")

Every accounting firm employs people to do work that software will increasingly be able to do. Coalesc is building that software.

## What I expected to find

I came to accounting from data. At Shopify, Wealthsimple and 1Password, I worked where information moved between systems on its own. So when I started spending time inside accounting firms, I assumed this part had been solved long ago. Documents are digital. Tax slips download straight from the CRA. Trial balances import with a click.

It hasn't been solved. It has moved.

Not long ago, accountants printed a client's trial balance and checked it line by line with a ruler, ticking off each number. Importing ended that. But the work didn't disappear. It moved into the steps in between, and those steps still decide how long it takes to produce what is often a document of only a few pages: a set of financial statements, or a tax return ready to sign.

## Two milestones

Receiving a client's documents and having a file ready for review are two very different milestones. Between them sits an enormous amount of work that rarely gets discussed outside the profession.

One accountant told me that 99% of the time, something is missing. What struck me wasn't the number. It was how much of the profession's time goes into figuring out what's missing, finding it and rebuilding the context needed to continue.

Documents arrive one email a day for two weeks: PDFs, spreadsheets, photos, a note the client typed into Word. Someone has to work out which client and which year each one belongs to, name it, file it, find what's still missing and chase the client. A preparer starts a file, discovers a gap halfway through, sets it aside, and later has to rebuild their understanding before continuing. When the file reaches review, one correction can send it back: a section to redo, taxes to recalculate, and the return, the provision and the financial statements to bring back in line.

At the end of all that, a surprisingly hard question remains: is this file actually ready for review?

The work doesn't stop at review either. Engagement letters, reporting letters and instalment letters are still produced from templates customized by hand. Signed authorizations have to be chased, collected and filed before anything can go out.

Much of this work is necessary. Some of it requires real professional judgment. But a significant portion is repetitive preparation that shouldn't need to be done by hand, again, every year.

## Why existing software stops short

Accounting firms aren't short on software. They have portals to collect documents, systems to store them and software to prepare returns and financial statements. AI can already do remarkable things inside each of them, sometimes finishing in minutes what used to take a day.

But moving information into a system isn't the same as getting the work done. What's missing are the connections: something that reads what came in, knows where it belongs, puts it there and tells someone the file is ready. Between the systems, people still do the work.

Even AI agents haven't closed that gap yet. A general-purpose assistant can work wonders inside a spreadsheet, but it can't carry a file from the inbox into the accounting software and on to review. The largest tax and accounting software companies are investing heavily in agents of their own. In the firms I've spent time with, none has delivered this yet. That's the gap Coalesc is built to close.

## What we're building

Coalesc is building the execution layer that carries an engagement from the moment client information arrives to the moment the finished work goes out for signature. It gathers and organizes what the client sends, identifies what's missing and follows up, enters and checks the numbers, prepares the file for review, and tracks what still has to come back signed. Every step carries its evidence, the questions still open and the exceptions that need a person.

The accountant's job moves from data entry to review and planning, which is where their expertise belongs.

It's already changing the arithmetic. One of our customers used to hire a full-time seasonal employee every tax season. Last season, they didn't need to. Coalesc saved them more than 85 hours in two months on a single step: getting finished returns to clients and the signed authorizations back.

## Bigger than paperwork

This is a much bigger opportunity than automating paperwork.

Accounting firms have always grown the same way: more clients means more staff. Every new engagement brings more preparation, more follow-up and more review cycles. Capacity has meant headcount.

If software can reliably handle a growing share of that work, the equation changes. A firm can serve significantly more clients without hiring proportionally. Its people spend their days on review, advice and decisions instead of assembling files. That's a different kind of firm, and I think it's where the profession is going.

## Why reliability is the whole game

What partners have told me, again and again, is that speed isn't enough. Their names are on the work. Thousands of files and the trust their firm has built over decades depend on it being correct, complete and traceable. Software that does the work has to show its work.

So we hold ourselves to a simple test: how much of what we prepare can an accountant accept as it is, and how much do they still have to fix or redo? Every point we move on that measure is capacity a firm gets back. That's why we build on real files with accounting teams, starting with tax, compilation and year-end work. The details that break a clean demo are the ones that matter.

## Where this goes

We're starting with tax, compilation and year-end because that's where the pressure is highest. But every engagement runs on the same thing: client information that has to become accounting work someone can stand behind. Once software can carry that reliably, it stops being a tool for one season and becomes the layer every engagement runs on.

Today, firms grow by hiring. I believe they'll grow by serving more clients with the people they have.

The more time I spend inside accounting firms, the more convinced I am that the biggest opportunity isn't to give accountants another AI assistant. It's to remove the repetitive work standing between their expertise and the clients who need it.

That's the company I want to build.
